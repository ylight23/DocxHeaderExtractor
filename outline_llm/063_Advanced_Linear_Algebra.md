So, for this purpose, doing mathematics in R3 , the best is to regard our space R3 as being part of a hierarchy of spaces RN , where you can do mathematics, at varying levels of difficulty, as follows: (1) First comes R. This has little to no interest in connection with real-life problems, but as you know well from calculus, everything mathematics comes from here, with this meaning sequences, series, functions, continuity, derivatives, integrals and so on. In this book we will assume the basic theory of R known. If you need from time to time to revise that, you have here Rudin [77], or Lax-Terrell [68], or the first part of my book [7]. (2) Then comes R2 . This is the entry point to advanced mathematics, because most of the R3 phenomenona have interesting 2D analogues, quite often capturing the whole point. Sometimes, R2 can be even your final destination, because many interesting R3 questions take place in fact in a plane R2 ⊂ R3 . And finally, as another key feature of R2 , we have an isomorphism R2 ≃ C, transforming by some kind of magic your 2-variable questions in R2 into routine 1-variable problems, over the complex numbers C. In this book we will assume R2 , C reasonably known, and in case you need from time to time a reference here, go with the books of Rudin [78], or Lax-Terrell [69], or mine [7]. (3) Then comes R3 . Here there are no tricks of type R2 ≃ C, so we are definitely into several variables, whose functioning we must understand well. But intuition, helped by the R3 surrounding us, can help a lot. - CHAPTER 13 Finite groups 13a. Finite groups We discuss in this final Part IV the various groups that the matrices can form. Let us start with some basic group theory. As a beginning for everything, we have: Definition 13.1. A group is a set G endowed with a multiplication operation (g, h) → gh which must satisfy the following conditions: (1) Associativity: we have, (gh)k = g(hk), for any g, h, k ∈ G. (2) Unit: there is an element 1 ∈ G such that g1 = 1g = g, for any g ∈ G. (3) Inverses: for any g ∈ G there is g −1 ∈ G such that gg −1 = g −1 g = 1. The multiplication law is not necessarily commutative.
